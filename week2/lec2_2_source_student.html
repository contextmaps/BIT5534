Analysis of Variance: One Test, Then Ten Comparisons
Duration: 75 minutes · Platform: Hokie AI · Submission: Thread exported as a .txt and uploaded to Canvas before leaving class · Lecture Mode: consolidation
Learning Objectives

By the end of this lecture, students will be able to:

  1. Recognise when an input is categorical and say why the fitted model is a set of group means and not a line.
  2. Decompose the total variation into a model part and an error part, and form the F ratio from them.
  3. Compute how many pairwise comparisons a factor with k levels produces, and the per-comparison error rate needed to hold the family at a chosen level.
  4. Follow the route from the F test through Levene to either Tukey HSD or Welch with Games-Howell, and say at each branch what runs next and what ends the analysis.
  5. Read a starter prompt closely enough to see how it names the input, justifies each test and sets its own boundary, then settle the decisions it deliberately left open.
  6. Read a connecting-letters report as an analyst, saying which claims it supports, which it will not support, and what they would put in front of a decision maker.
Section 1 of 5
INTRODUCTION
📋 Note for Students

Last class the input was continuous and the model was a line. Today the input is a category, so there is no slope to fit. The model becomes one number for each level, and the question becomes whether those numbers are far enough apart to be worth believing.

Every row in today's data is one person. They were asked to process a list of words in one of five ways, and the number of words they recalled was recorded. The input is which way they processed, which is a category and not a number. Everything today follows from that one change.

Read the middle term. If every person in a group recalled exactly that group's mean, the model would explain everything and the error term would be zero. It never is. What matters is the size of one against the other.

Section 2 of 5
DEMONSTRATION

The alternative is why the session does not end here. The F test tells you that a difference exists somewhere among the five levels. It will not tell you which pair, and the obvious next move, testing every pair, is where the trouble starts.

Hold that number. A single comparison at 0.05 feels ordinary. Ten of them at 0.05 each give you better than a one in three chance of calling at least one difference that is not there, and pulling the family back to 0.05 means each comparison has to clear about 0.005 instead. In section 4 you will watch two specific comparisons fail to clear it.

Ten comparisons are hard to read and easy to misreport, so the result is usually written as connecting letters instead. Every group gets one or more letters, and two groups share a letter when the evidence does not separate them.

A group can carry two letters, which is the part that gets misread. In the panel above, Group 2 is not separated from Group 1 and is not separated from Group 3, while Group 1 and Group 3 are separated from each other. The letters describe how much evidence there is. The ends sit far enough apart to call and the middle does not.

The F test asks whether the factor matters at all. Levene asks a different question, whether the groups have similar spread, and it decides two things. It decides which overall test you trust, meaning the ordinary F test or Welch, and it decides which comparison procedure produces your letters. Tukey pools all the groups to estimate one variance. Games-Howell estimates a variance for each pair, which is what lets it hold when the spreads differ. Two of these tests can end the analysis before any comparison happens, and this route is the thing you are about to build into a tool.

Run your tool on the data file and read what comes back. Everyone in this room is running one dataset through one route, so the numbers on your screen are the numbers on everyone else's. What you take from them is not.

Three things AND in this order. Say which claim your letters support, meaning which levels you could tell a client are genuinely apart AND say which claim they refuse, meaning two levels whose means differ on the page and which your report will not let you separate AND say what the spread adds, if your report prints a standard deviation for each group, meaning which level varies the most and whether that is the level you would have picked.

The second one is where most readings go wrong. A group with a higher mean is not a better group when it shares a letter with the one below it, and a letter report is not a ranking. The third one is where the analysis is. Two methods can reach nearly the same average and behave nothing alike, because one of them lands anywhere between five and twenty-three while the other stays close to its average, and a decision maker choosing between them cares about that more than about the gap in the means.

Answer both. The survey carries no credit and is here so the room can see whether one output read by sixty-nine analysts produces one reading.
