Learning Objectives

By the end of this lecture, students will be able to:

  1. Compute the SS of a group of rows and the SS reduction of a cut, and say why a regression tree keeps the cut with the largest reduction.
  2. Work one split in full by hand, from the target values to the reduction.
  3. Extend a working decision tree tool to regression trees from a short specification.
  4. Read a regression tree's leaf report and R squared history, and choose a number of splits and a minimum leaf size to stop at.
  5. Read column contributions and say what a contribution of zero does and does not show.
  6. Say what a leaf mean tells you about one new student, and what it cannot tell you.

INTRODUCTION
📋 Note for Students

Tuesday's tree asked who comes back for a second year. Today's asks a number. Same hundred freshmen, same file, and the target is GPA.

Almost everything you built on Tuesday carries over. One thing changes, and it is how a cut is scored.

Same hundred freshmen as Tuesday. The target is now GPA, and a GPA is a number.

When a leaf has to predict a number, what should it predict?

A leaf predicts the mean GPA of the students in it. How wrong that is, across the leaf, is the leaf's SS, the squared distance of every student's GPA from the leaf's mean, added up.

A cut splits a leaf into two smaller ones, each with its own mean. The cut is worth whatever SS it takes away.

Worked at the root. All hundred students have a mean GPA of 2.216 and an SS of 78.38.

The best cut is 140.5 miles from home. Below it, 24 students with a mean of 2.838 and an SS of 16.43. Above it, 76 students with a mean of 2.020 and an SS of 49.72.

The reduction is 78.38 minus 16.43 minus 49.72, which is 12.22.

Then the recursion, unchanged from Tuesday. The 24 and the 76 are each a file of their own, and the tree runs the same search inside each of them and splits the leaf whose best cut takes away the most.

Section 4 of 5
DISCUSSION

Three questions, and your own tool answers all of them.

First, run it on freshmen.csv with gpa as the target, return out of the inputs and one split. Your tool may print the cut differently. What matters is how many students land on each side and what R squared reads.

Now step to four splits and read your leaf report against the tree in section 2. R squared should read 0.383.

Second, open Column contributions. High school GPA contributes nothing. Set the minimum leaf size to 5 and step to nine splits, and look again.

Third, where to stop.

The first two splits carry 0.277 of the R squared. The next ten add 0.346 between them, and some of those splits rest on two or three students. A minimum leaf size of 5 rules the smallest of them out before they are tried.

Every number on this curve was measured on the same hundred students the tree was grown from. How would you know whether a split holds for next year's freshmen?
