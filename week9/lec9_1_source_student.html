Performance Measures and the Confusion Matrix
Duration: 75 minutes · Platform: Hokie AI · Submission: Thread exported as a .zip and uploaded to Canvas before leaving class · Lecture Mode: exploratory
Learning Objectives

By the end of this lecture, students will be able to:

  1. Compute the error, MSE, RMSE and MAD of a model for a number target, and compare a model with the mean of the training rows through relative RMSE and relative MAD.
  2. Build the confusion matrix of a classifier from its actual and predicted classes, and name TP, FP, FN and TN.
  3. Divide the matrix by its row sums to get TPR, FNR, FPR and TNR, and by its column sums to get precision, and give each measure's other names.
  4. Compute F1 from precision and recall and explain why it is a harmonic mean.
  5. Move a threshold on saved probabilities, read how the four counts change, and choose a threshold by which error costs more.
  6. Say to a classmate which threshold they would choose and which error set it.
Section 1 of 5
INTRODUCTION
📋 Note for Students

Every model since week 2 has been judged by one number on its own scale, R squared for a number target and misclassification for a category. Weeks 9 and 10 compare models properly, and that starts with the measures underneath those single numbers.

Today runs on the kind of file you built on Thursday. diabetes_saved.csv holds the four saved predictions on Y and the four saved probabilities of High on Y Binary, made by Thursday's four tools with the same k fold column and seed 123, so every figure on this page comes from the same 133 test rows.

Start with Y, the disease progression score, a number. For every test row the error is the actual value minus the predicted value. The left panel shows the regression's errors on nineteen test rows. The right panel shows the errors of the simplest possible prediction, the mean of the training rows, which needs no model at all.

Three ways to add the errors up. MSE averages the squared errors, so one large miss outweighs several small ones. RMSE is its square root, back on the scale of Y. MAD averages the absolute errors and treats every unit of error alike.

An RMSE of 56 on its own says little until it is set beside something. The relative measures divide a model's figure by the figure of the training mean. A value below 1 is the share of the mean's error that remains, and a value of 1 means the model does no better than predicting 151 for every patient.

Before the category target:

  • The regression's RMSE is 56.1 and its MAD is 45.1. What would have to be true of its errors for the two to be equal?
  • The tree's relative RMSE is 0.833. How much of the mean's error does the tree remove?
Section 2 of 5
DEMONSTRATION

Now Y Binary, with High as the positive class. Take the regression's saved probability of High and call every patient at or above 0.5 a predicted High. Two sets appear inside the 133 test rows, the patients who are actually High and the patients the model predicts High, and four regions with them.

Lay the four regions out as cells and the Venn becomes the confusion matrix. Rows hold the actual class and columns the predicted class. Divide each row by its sum and you get rates within the actual class. Divide each column by its sum and you get rates within the predicted class.

Divide each column by its sum instead and you get rates within the predicted class. PREC, precision, is the share of the patients the model calls High who are actually High. Recall and precision share their numerator, TP, and F1 joins them with a harmonic mean, which sits near the smaller of the two.
