Learning Objectives

By the end of this lecture, students will be able to:

  1. Describe how a transformer turns a ticket into word pieces, passes them through layers of self-attention, and returns one vector for the whole ticket.
  2. Explain why the meaning of a word in a transformer depends on its context, and why a bag of words gives the same word one column wherever it appears.
  3. Compare cosine similarity on tf-idf rows with cosine similarity on embeddings for tickets that describe one problem in different words.
  4. Describe BERTopic's three steps, UMAP, HDBSCAN and c-TF-IDF, and read a topic from its words and its outliers.
  5. Use topic scores from NMF and from BERTopic as inputs to a regression for a category target and for a number target, and read the inputs by logworth.
Section 1 of 5
INTRODUCTION
📋 Note for Students

On Tuesday the bag of words turned 600 help desk tickets into columns, and those columns predicted escalation better than the site did. It also missed things. Two tickets about one sign in problem, written in different words, looked unrelated, and a topic built on register and store mixed broken registers with registers that could not connect.

Today replaces the counts with embeddings from a transformer model, all-MiniLM-L6-v2, which reads every word in the context of the words around it. The same tickets, the same tool and the same week 8 regression let you see what changes.

A bag of words gives every word one column, wherever it appears. The register column is the same in a ticket about a cracked register screen and in a ticket about a register that cannot reach the payment server. Tuesday's T1 put register and registers in one topic, and it mixed broken registers with registers that could not connect.

People read a word through its neighbors. In failure to obey school bus stop sign, a line from a traffic citation, bus alone could be a vehicle or a data bus, and school and stop settle it. A transformer builds that reading into the numbers. Each word ends up as a vector shaped by the words around it, so register beside cracked and register beside connect become different vectors.

Section 2 of 5
DEMONSTRATION

The model behind today's embeddings is all-MiniLM-L6-v2, a small member of the BERT family. BERT stands for bidirectional encoder representations from transformers, and bidirectional means each word is read with the words on both sides of it. The model first splits text into word pieces from a fixed vocabulary of 30,522, so a rare word becomes several pieces and an unknown word is never dropped. Authenticator becomes authentic and ##ator, and checkout becomes check and ##out.

Self-attention is where a word takes in its neighbors. In each layer every piece scores every piece, itself included, turns the scores into weights that add to one, and adds a weighted mix of their value vectors to its own vector. In the figure, from the last of the six layers, register beside a cracked screen gives its largest share to cracked, and registers beside a payment server gives its largest to payment. In the traffic citation, bus gives its largest share to school.

After the six layers, the word carries its context. The register vector from the cracked screen sentence and the one from a second broken register sentence, The register at checkout two will not turn on, have a cosine of 0.938. Against registers in the payment server sentence it is 0.855, though registers is also a different piece. In a bag of words, the register column in both broken register sentences is identical.

The ticket's embedding is the average of its pieces after the last layer, scaled to length 1, so the cosine of two tickets is again the sum of products of their numbers, now 384 numbers in place of 1235 term weights. Tuesday's sign in tickets are the test. On tf-idf two tickets with different wordings averaged 0.046. On embeddings they average 0.410, against 0.531 for two tickets with the same wording. Against tickets about other problems the embedding cosine is about 0.24, so 0.410 sits well above that floor.

Neighbors change the same way. On Tuesday KP10031, a picker who cannot log in to the warehouse system, had one sign in ticket among its five tf-idf neighbors. On embeddings it has two, KP10184 at 0.665 and KP10517 at 0.523, and KP10277, KP10392 and KP10032 are warehouse tickets about access and registers. The word warehouse still pulls, because the model encodes the whole ticket, setting included.

BERTopic finds topics on the embeddings in three steps. UMAP reduces the 384 numbers to five while keeping each ticket's neighbors. HDBSCAN then finds dense groups in those five dimensions and leaves any ticket in no group as an outlier, so the number of topics comes from the data. Last, c-TF-IDF joins the tickets of each topic into one document and weights its words, so a topic is named by words that are common in it and rare in the others.

The topics are also inputs to a model. Tuesday's NMF gave each ticket six topic weights, numbers from 0 up. BERTopic gives each ticket one topic, a category column named BT_topic, and a cosine with each topic's center, BT_0 to BT_8 on this run. reg_explorer.py fits a logistic regression when the target is a category, here escalated, and a multiple regression when the target is a number, here hours_to_resolve, and it turns a category input into one column per topic after the first.

Logworth, minus the log10 of a term's p value, is the evidence that a term's effect is not zero, and a topic's effect is measured against a BT0 ticket. For hours the largest is BT2, laptop, screen and power, at 14.6, with an estimate of 7.6 hours more than a BT0 ticket. The password topic, BT7, has an estimate of 4.6 hours less. NMF's six weights reach a logworth of 4.3 at most, and each of them is a slope per unit of weight.

Section 4 of 5
DISCUSSION
💬 Discussion

Take these into the reflection:

  • BT_topic beat the six NMF weights on both targets. What can a topic built from embeddings tell about a ticket that a topic built from counts cannot?
  • On the page, the cosine scores BT_0 to BT_8 did worse than BT_topic, though both enter the model as nine columns. Look at what a cosine to a topic center measures for a ticket far from every center, and say what the single topic label keeps that the scores blur.
  • Most outliers are hardware tickets about headsets, fans and webcams. Would you route an outlier automatically, send it to a person, or give it a topic of its own?

Turn to the person beside you for three minutes. Each of you names one thing the embeddings saw in today's tickets that the bag of words missed AND one thing they cost you, from what you ran today. A model's inputs are a choice, and naming what each input cannot see is how the choice is defended.
