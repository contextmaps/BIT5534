K-Means Clustering, A Method That Answers Differently Each Time
Platform: Hokie AI (build) + local Python (run) · Submission: Thread exported as a .zip and uploaded to Canvas
Learning Objectives

By the end of this part, students will be able to:

  1. Report the number of clusters k-means selects by silhouette and the scores on either side of it.
  2. Run the same analysis five times with one restart and report how much the answer moved.
  3. Say what a method that answers differently each time costs a business, and what the restart count buys.
  4. Name the columns that make a k-means cluster prominent and say what a store in that cluster would be like.
  5. Carry a number found by one method into another method and hold it fixed on purpose.
  6. Compare two clusterings of the same file at the same k by their scores, their cluster sizes and their profiles, and count how many rows changed group.
  7. Say what a comparison settles and what it leaves open.

Same 125 stores, same six columns, different method. Part 1 built a tree and cut it. K-means never builds a tree. It drops k centres into the data at random, assigns every store to the nearest one, moves each centre to the middle of what it caught, and repeats until nothing moves.

The word random in that sentence is the whole of case one. A tree is the same tree every time you build it. K-means is not, and the tool handles that by running the whole search many times from different random starts and keeping the best. How many times is a setting, and case one is where you find out what that setting is worth.

Case two is the comparison. You take the number of clusters the best linkage found in part 1, hold it fixed, and put the two methods side by side on the same file at the same k.

CASE ONE

Case one is worth 50 points, 35 for the exchange in the M block and 15 for the reflection.

Open kmeans_explorer.py, load retail_stores.csv, take all six numeric columns, set store as the label column, leave standardize on, set k from 2 to 10, and leave restarts per k at 50.

Check two numbers before you read anything else. The silhouette peaks at five clusters with a score of 0.5196, and the five clusters hold 38, 31, 31, 15 and 10 stores. If your screen agrees, the tool is computing what it claims to compute on the file this page is written about.

Then find the restarts box. It is set to 50 because Thursday's tool was built that way, and a setting nobody has questioned is a setting nobody has measured.

Four moves, in order. The card below carries them.

Move one claims the k at fifty restarts AND move two measures what happens at one AND move three claims three profiles AND move four works through the verdict. All four.

Move one opens the block. Attach retail_stores.csv and write a paragraph that says four things: that you have already run this analysis yourself in kmeans_explorer, that you are going to state what you observed and want it checked against the data, that it should tell you for each claim whether you are right, wrong, overstating or understating, and that it should not describe the analysis ahead of you or list results you have not asked about. Then in the same message, claim the number of clusters the silhouette selects and give the scores on either side of it.

Move two is the measurement, and it is the part of this homework that has no equivalent in part 1. Set restarts per k to 1. Run the analysis five times, changing the seed each time, and write down two things on every run: the number of clusters with the best silhouette, and the silhouette at five clusters. Then set restarts back to 50 and run once more. Report all six runs in the thread as a short list, and say what the extra restarts bought.

Move three claims three profiles. Pick three of your clusters from the 50 restart run and for each one name the columns that stand out, saying which are high and which are low, then say what a store in that cluster would actually be like for those columns to sit where they do. A cluster that is highest on markdown and lowest on loyalty is a description. A store selling inventory down to people who do not come back is a reading, and the reading is what is being asked for.

Move four reads the verdict on all of it. Where it says you are overstating, ask what in the data makes it an overstatement. Where you think it is wrong, say so and hold your ground until it gives you a number.

CASE TWO

Case two is worth 50 points, 35 for the exchange in the M block and 15 for the reflection.

In part 1 the five linkages disagreed about how many clusters the file holds. In case one of this part k-means gave its own answer. Case two stops asking how many and asks a different question: given the same number, do the two methods put the same stores together?

Open your part 1 silhouette table. One linkage scored highest, and the number of clusters it selected is the number you are going to hold fixed here. Set k-means to that number only, with restarts at 50, and run it. Then open hier_explorer.py, load the same file with the same six columns, set that linkage and that number of clusters, and leave it on screen.

You now have two clusterings of 125 stores into the same number of groups, made by two methods that share nothing but the data. Both tools will save the cluster number for every store to a csv. Use those two files.

One thing to sort out before you count anything. The two tools number their clusters independently, so cluster 3 in one is not cluster 3 in the other. Match them by profile first, pairing the group that is highest on markdown with the group that is highest on markdown, and so on down. Only once the pairs are settled does counting how many stores moved mean anything.

Four moves, in order. The card below carries them.

Move one carries the number across AND move two compares the scores and the sizes AND move three compares the profiles and counts what moved AND move four says what the comparison settled. All four.

Move one opens the block. Attach retail_stores.csv and set the terms as you did in case one. Then say which linkage scored highest in your part 1 table, at how many clusters and with what silhouette, and say that you are holding that number fixed for k-means. Give the k-means silhouette you get at that k.

Move two compares the two numbers and the two shapes. Put the two silhouettes side by side and say which is higher and by how much. Put the two lists of cluster sizes side by side and say how close they are.

Move three is the work. Save the cluster numbers from both tools, match the groups by profile, and count how many of the 125 stores are in a different group under one method than the other. Report the count. Then look at the two parallel coordinates plots and say whether the same five profiles appear in both.

Move four says what the comparison settled and what it did not. The two methods disagreed in part 1 and you have now seen what they do when the disagreement is removed.
