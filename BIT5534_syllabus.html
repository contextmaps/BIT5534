Course Description

Development of business intelligence and analytics solutions and applications for decision-making problems. Analytics software and techniques, data preparation, data exploration and visualization, predictive analytics techniques, text analytics. The course has been rebuilt around how this work is actually done now. Rather than moving through fixed menu sequences in a statistical package, you specify each method in plain language, build it as a working Python tool with an AI assistant, verify the tool against a result you can check, and spend the time you save on what the output means and what a business should do about it. The semester closes with a team-built business intelligence and analytics dashboard presented to the class.

Course Objectives

By the end of the semester, students will be able to:

  1. Specify an analytics method in operational language, build it as a working Python tool with an AI assistant, and verify the tool against a known result before trusting anything it produces.
  2. Choose among regression, classification, dimension reduction, segmentation, and ensemble methods for a given business decision problem, and say why the alternatives were set aside.
  3. Read model output for what it says about the data and the decision, rather than for whether a number cleared a threshold.
  4. Build an honest evaluation harness, compare candidate models on metrics tied to the cost of the decision, and defend the model selected.
  5. Recognize the ways an AI assistant fails on technical material, and verify generated code and generated explanations before they reach a result.
  6. Design and build a business intelligence and analytics dashboard that delivers a decision to a non-technical audience.
  7. Present the dashboard and the analysis behind it to peers, and defend the choices it rests on.

How This Course Works: Thread-Centered Pedagogy

This course is taught through Thread-Centered Pedagogy. In every session you open a conversation with HokieAI, work through a sequence of structured blocks on the course companion, build and run what the session calls for, and submit the whole conversation together with whatever your tools produced. That conversation is your thread, and the thread is the work. Four things follow from that.

You write your own prompts. The companion supplies structure, not sentences. Where a block asks you to specify a method, interrogate an explanation, push back on an answer, or reflect on what happened, the words are yours. This matters most for the reflection that closes each session. Asking the assistant to draft your reflection removes the only part of the session that records your own thinking, and it will be treated as a submission that is not your own.

All of your work stays in the thread. Work done outside the thread cannot be assessed, because the thread is the record. Do not delete turns, do not edit messages you have already sent, and do not reconstruct a conversation after the fact. A thread that shows you getting stuck, catching an error in generated code, or changing your mind about a model is worth more than a tidy one.

Generated code is your responsibility. The assistant writes a great deal of the Python in this course, and it will sometimes write code that runs and is wrong. Every session that builds a tool also gives you something to check it against. A result you did not verify is not a result, and a thread that shows the check is what separates the two.

The thread is transparent and traceable. Your submission shows what you asked, what came back, what you ran, and what you did next. That is what separates honest AI-assisted analysis from analysis whose origin nobody can see, and building that habit is one of the reasons this course exists in its current form.

How submissions are evaluated. Each block is assessed against the instruction printed above it on the companion page, and every graded element produces a distribution across the whole class. A thread that was not genuinely had does not fall where real threads fall in those distributions. It becomes visible without anyone having to go looking for it.

If a submission is not genuine. A first instance receives zero points for that submission. Any further instance is reported to the Graduate Honor System. A student who disputes a case will be given a comparable but independent assignment to complete under direct supervision. If the quality of that interaction differs substantially from the disputed submission, the case is reported to the Honor System with the supervised result submitted alongside the original.

Course Text

There is no required textbook. Everything you need is delivered through the course companion and by the instructor. Students who want a conventional reference alongside the course may use Fundamentals of Predictive Analytics with JMP, Second Edition, by Klimberg and McCullough, which covers most of the methods in the outline below.

Software Required

HokieAI, signed in with your Virginia Tech credentials, at hokieai.vt.edu. It is the only AI platform used in this course. You also need a working Python installation on your own machine, which you will set up in week 1 with the assistant's help. JMP Student Edition, at software.vt.edu/getjmpse, remains available and is useful for cross-checking a result you have just computed yourself. Nothing in this course has to be purchased.

Computer Equipment

You must bring a laptop to every class meeting. It needs to connect to the internet, run Python locally, and meet the recommended configuration for students entering the Pamplin College of Business. Every session is hands-on and cannot be completed without it. An extension cable is worth carrying so that a low battery does not cost you a submission.

Lectures and Classwork

Lectures are in person and every one of them is hands-on. Session material appears on the companion at the start of class. You work through the blocks during the meeting, run what you build, and submit your thread together with any files your tools produced before you leave. Twenty-four graded sessions run from week 2 through week 13 at 1.5 points each. Week 1 introduces the workflow and sets up Python, and is not graded, which is deliberate: the first week is for learning how the course works, not for being measured on it.

Homework Assignments

Nine homework assignments, one for each of weeks 2 through 10, each worth 3 points. Each is released at the end of the day on Thursday and is due at 11:59 pm on the Friday of the following week. Homework has the same shape as classwork and is usually more substantial, and some assignments ask for more than one thread. Homework is individual work. Discussing homework with classmates is allowed and encouraged, and a submission that reproduces another student's thread or another student's code in whole or in part is not. A submission up to 24 hours late is subject to a 10 percent penalty and receives no credit after that. Once a homework grade is released, you have one week to raise a question about it.

Project

Teams of four to five students design and build a business intelligence and analytics dashboard for an authentic business decision problem. Teams form informally in week 5 and are finalized in week 6. Three deliverables are due during the semester at 10 points each: the problem and its data, the analysis and model selection, and the dashboard itself. Each team presents its dashboard during week 14 for 10 points, evaluated by the other teams. Weeks 12 and 13 are in-class development sessions given over to the build. Full project instructions are released in week 5.

Absences from Class

A student who has to miss a lecture for a documentable reason can request an extension on that session's classwork until 11:59 pm the same day. Request it by email to seref@vt.edu at least 24 hours before the lecture begins. Requests after that deadline will be dismissed. Supporting documentation goes to the Excuse Document Submission assignment on Canvas as a PDF. Official activities such as school-related travel and professional activities such as job interviews are valid reasons, recognized on submission of a letter or email from the relevant authority or organization. For a health problem, notify the instructor by email before the lecture where possible and no later than three days after it, with documentation submitted within the same three days. Other emergencies are handled case by case and should be raised as soon as possible. Personal preferences, including family travel and leaving early for or returning late from a break, are not valid reasons. An accepted excuse grants an extension and nothing more: the work still has to be completed by the extended deadline to earn credit.

AI-Based Technologies

Using AI is expected in every session of this course, which makes the rules different from most of your other classes. HokieAI is the only approved platform, and using another AI service for coursework requires the instructor's approval in advance. What is unacceptable here is not the use of AI. It is submitting a thread you did not have, in any form: a conversation written for you, a thread edited after the fact to look better than it was, code presented as verified that was never run, or a reflection drafted by the assistant and presented as your own thinking. See the section on Thread-Centered Pedagogy above for how submissions are evaluated and what happens when one is not genuine.
