Decision Trees on Loan Default, With and Without Credit Bureau Data
Platform: Hokie AI (conversation) + local Python (run) · Submission: Thread exported as a .zip and uploaded to Canvas
Learning Objectives

By the end of this part, students will be able to:

  1. Grow a decision tree on a business file and report its first split, its riskiest and safest leaves, and the rule that defines each.
  2. Turn a leaf's rule into an action a lending manager could take, and say what the rule cannot tell that manager.
  3. Explain why a column that scores well at the root can contribute nothing to the finished tree.
  4. Remove a group of columns on purpose, grow the tree again, and name the columns that step in for them.
  5. Compare two trees by the rules they share, the rules that disappear and how sharply their extreme leaves separate.
  6. Make a recommendation about buying data, ground it in rules from their own trees, and defend it against a challenge.

Both cases work on the same file. It holds 1,000 past loan applicants at a regional bank, with 14 columns. Default is 1 for an applicant who defaulted and 0 for one who repaid. Credit_Score and Credit_Utilization_Ratio come from a credit bureau, which the bank pays for every applicant it checks. The other eleven columns are ones the bank already has, from the application form and from its own records.

Case one is this week's method on a business file, and the reading is the work. You grow the tree, and then you read it the way a lending manager would, as rules someone could act on tomorrow.

Case two asks a question the bank actually faces. The bureau subscription costs money on every application. You grow the same tree without the two bureau columns and decide what the bank would lose by dropping them.

A decision tree is not the most accurate model there is, and this homework does not ask you to make it one. Its value is that it hands you rules a person can read and act on, which is what both cases are about.

CASE ONE

Case one is worth 50 points, 35 for the exchange in the M block and 15 for the reflection.

Open tree_explorer_regression.py and load loan_dataset.csv. The tool picks Default as the target, because it is the first column, and selects every other column as an input. Leave all thirteen inputs selected, Credit_Score included. The list shows twelve at a time, so scroll inside it to see Num_Dependents at the bottom. Replace the 1 in Minimum leaf size with 10 and press Enter. Then set Number of splits to 6 and press Enter, or press Grow one split until the box reads 6.

Check three things before you read anything else. The coloured label under Minimum leaf size should say Decision tree. The Split history tab should be titled Entropy R squared 0.311 at 6 splits. The Leaf report should list seven leaves. If all three agree, the tool is computing what it claims to compute on the file this page is written about.

The Leaf report is where the rules are. Each row is one leaf, with the count of applicants who repaid under 0, the count who defaulted under 1, and the full rule from the root. The default rate of a leaf is the count under 1 divided by the two counts together. When a rule names the same column twice, such as Credit_Score < 648 AND Credit_Score < 590, the tighter cut is the one that binds. A rule such as Employment_Status not in {Employed, Retired, Self-employed, Student} is the tool's way of naming the one level left out, which here is Unemployed.

Four moves, in order. The card below carries them.

Move one reports the tree AND move two turns two rules into actions AND move three explains a column that scored well and contributes nothing AND move four works through what Hokie AI disputes. All four.

Move one opens the block. Write in your prompt that you grew this tree yourself in a tool Hokie AI cannot see, that you will report what it shows, and that you want each reading tested and not repeated back to you. Then report the tree. Write the first split, then the riskiest leaf and the safest leaf, each with its full rule, its count and its default rate.

Move two reads the rules as a lending manager would. Pick two leaves and write, for each, what the bank could do with its rule tomorrow, such as approve, decline, price the loan higher or ask for more documents. Then ask Hokie AI whether the rule supports that action and what it cannot tell the manager.

Move three looks underneath the tree. Set Number of splits to 0 and press Enter, so the whole file is one leaf, and open the Candidates tab, which now ranks every column's best cut at the root. Then set Number of splits back to 6 and open Column contributions. One column scores second best at the root and contributes nothing to the finished tree. Write in your prompt which column it is, with its score at the root, and your explanation of why the tree never used it. Ask Hokie AI to test the explanation.

Move four is where the exchange earns its place. Where Hokie AI says you are reading too much into a rule, ask what a correctly stated version would be. Where you think it is wrong, say so and give it the number from your tool that settles it. You can see the tree and it cannot, so a disagreement is yours to settle.

One move is not one message. Ten messages inside the block is the ceiling, and a ceiling is not a target.

CASE TWO

Case two is worth 50 points, 35 for the exchange in the M block and 15 for the reflection.

The bank pays the credit bureau for Credit_Score and Credit_Utilization_Ratio on every application. A manager has proposed dropping the subscription and deciding from what the bank already has, the application form and its own records. Your job is to say what the bank would lose, using two trees you grew yourself.

Leave the target on Default. In the Input columns list, click Credit_Score and Credit_Utilization_Ratio to unselect them, and leave the other eleven selected. Keep Minimum leaf size at 10 and Number of splits at 6, and press Enter in the splits box so the tree regrows.

Check two things. The Split history tab should be titled Entropy R squared 0.131 at 6 splits. The first split in the Leaf report rules should be on Debt_to_Income_Ratio. If both agree, read on.

The tool prints cuts with four significant figures, so large numbers come out in scientific notation. Annual_Income < 2.071e+04 means an income below 20,710, and Loan_Amount_Requested < 1.049e+04 means a request below 10,490.

Keep your case one numbers beside you, from your thread or from notes. The comparison is the case.

Four moves, in order. The card below carries them.

Move one frames the decision and reports the new tree AND move two compares the two trees rule by rule AND move three makes a recommendation AND move four defends it against the other side. All four.

Move one opens the block. Write in your prompt the business question in your own words, then report the new tree. Give the first split, the columns that now appear in the rules, and the riskiest and safest leaves with their rules, counts and default rates.

Move two compares. Write which columns stepped in for the bureau data, which rules from case one survive in some form, and which have no stand-in at all. Then compare the extremes. Write the default rates of the riskiest and safest leaves in each tree, side by side, and say what the difference means for a manager deciding on an application. Ask Hokie AI to test your comparison.

Move three recommends. Write what you would tell the manager, keep the subscription, drop it, or something in between, such as buying the bureau report only for applicants the form cannot separate. Give the rule from your own trees that your recommendation rests on.

Move four argues the other side. Ask Hokie AI to make the strongest case against your recommendation, then answer it. Change your recommendation if the case is good, and say why if it is not.

One move is not one message. Twelve messages inside the block is the ceiling, and a ceiling is not a target.
