Regression Trees on Credit Scores, One Model or Two
Platform: Hokie AI (conversation) + local Python (run) · Submission: Thread exported as a .zip and uploaded to Canvas
Learning Objectives

By the end of this part, students will be able to:

  1. Grow a regression tree on a business file and report its first split, its highest and lowest scoring leaves, and the rule that defines each.
  2. Separate the columns in a tree's rules into those a customer can change and those they cannot, and say what a tree's rule does and does not show about cause.
  3. Find where a customer segment sits inside a tree grown for everyone, and say what that tree says about the segment.
  4. Grow a tree on the segment alone and name the columns that drive it.
  5. Compare the two trees by their drivers and their rules, without leaning on fit numbers that are not comparable.
  6. Recommend one scoring model or two, ground it in rules from their own trees, and defend it against a challenge.

CASE ONE

Case one is worth 50 points, 35 for the exchange in the M block and 15 for the reflection.

Open tree_explorer_regression.py and load loan_dataset.csv. Choose Credit_Score in the Target column box. The tool selects every other column as an input, Default included, so click Default in the Input columns list to unselect it. Whether an applicant defaulted is known only after the loan, long after the score was pulled, so it cannot explain the score. Leave the other twelve selected. The list shows twelve at a time, so scroll inside it to check the bottom. Replace the 1 in Minimum leaf size with 10 and press Enter. Then set Number of splits to 6 and press Enter.

Check three things before you read anything else. The coloured label under Minimum leaf size should say Regression tree. The Split history tab should be titled R squared 0.683 at 6 splits. The Leaf report should list seven leaves. If all three agree, read on.

Each row of the Leaf report is one leaf, with its count, its mean score, the standard deviation of the scores in it, and the full rule from the root.

Four moves, in order. The card below carries them.

Move one reports the tree AND move two separates what an applicant can change from what they cannot AND move three finds the younger applicants in the tree AND move four works through what Hokie AI disputes. All four.

Move one opens the block. Write in your prompt that you grew this tree yourself in a tool Hokie AI cannot see, that you will report what it shows, and that you want each reading tested and not repeated back to you. Then report the tree. Write the first split, then the highest scoring leaf and the lowest scoring leaf, each with its full rule, its count and its mean score.

Move two reads the rules from the applicant's side. Sort the columns in the rules into those an applicant could change within a year and those they cannot. Then write one piece of advice the bank could give an applicant who wants a higher score, built from a rule in your tree. Ask Hokie AI whether the rule supports the advice, and what a tree's rule can and cannot say about cause.

Move three looks for the younger applicants. Two leaves point to them. One has Age below 30 in its rule. The other has fewer than 3.5 credit accounts, which mostly means young applicants who have had less time to open them. Find both, write their rules, counts and means, and write what the tree says about younger applicants and how much of the tree is about them. Ask Hokie AI what a tree grown for everyone might miss about a group this size.

Move four is where the exchange earns its place. Where Hokie AI says you are reading too much into a rule, ask what a correctly stated version would be. Where you think it is wrong, say so and give it the number from your tool that settles it.

One move is not one message. Ten messages inside the block is the ceiling, and a ceiling is not a target.

CASE TWO

Case two is worth 50 points, 35 for the exchange in the M block and 15 for the reflection.

The bank's marketing team wants to reach young applicants with little credit history. Before it does, someone has to decide whether the bank's single scoring model treats them well, or whether they need a model of their own. Your job is to answer that with two trees, the one from case one and one grown on the segment.

Open thin_file.csv in the tool. It holds the 161 applicants from the same file who are under 30 and have been with the bank for under 3 years. Opening a new file resets the target to Default, so choose Credit_Score as the target again and unselect Default again. Keep Minimum leaf size at 10 and Number of splits at 6, and press Enter in the splits box.

Check two things. The status line should say 161 rows used. The Split history tab should be titled R squared 0.717 at 6 splits. If both agree, read on.

Do not compare the two R squared values as if one tree were better. They are measured on different groups of applicants, with different spreads of scores, so the numbers do not line up. Compare what drives each tree and what its rules say.

Four moves, in order. The card below carries them.

Move one frames the decision and reports the segment tree AND move two compares the drivers and rules of the two trees AND move three recommends one model or two AND move four defends it against the other side. All four.

Move one opens the block. Write in your prompt the business question in your own words, then report the segment tree. Give the first split, the columns with a contribution above zero and their percentages, and the highest and lowest scoring leaves with their rules, counts and means.

Move two compares. For every column that contributes in either tree, write its percentage in both trees side by side, with 0 where it does not appear, and say which column matters most in each. Then take one rule from the case one tree and say whether it would serve a thin file applicant well, using the segment tree as your evidence. Ask Hokie AI to test the comparison, and to say how much weight a leaf of about 20 applicants can carry.

Move three recommends. Write what you would tell the bank, one model for everyone or a separate model for thin file applicants, and give the rules from your own trees that your recommendation rests on. Say what a thin file applicant would be told differently under your recommendation.

Move four argues the other side. Ask Hokie AI to make the strongest case against your recommendation, then answer it. Change your recommendation if the case is good, and say why if it is not.

One move is not one message. Twelve messages inside the block is the ceiling, and a ceiling is not a target.
