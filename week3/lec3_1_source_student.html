Logistic Regression: Fitting a Line to Something That Is Not a Line
Duration: 75 minutes · Platform: Hokie AI · Submission: Thread exported as a .txt and uploaded to Canvas before leaving class · Lecture Mode: exploratory
Learning Objectives

By the end of this lecture, students will be able to:

  1. Say why a straight line is the wrong model for a binary response, in terms of the values it returns and the spread of its errors.
  2. Move between probability, odds, and log odds in both directions, and say which of the three the model is linear in.
  3. Read a logistic regression output and say what a slope does to the log odds and to the odds.
  4. Specify a single-input logistic regression tool in conversation and direct it to a finished prompt and a working script.
  5. Interpret the same model with a binary input and with a continuous input, and say what each one can and cannot tell them.
  6. Say what the whole-model test is in logistic regression and what its statistic is measuring.
Section 1 of 5
INTRODUCTION
📋 Note for Students

Last week every number in the regression output came from somewhere you could point at. Least squares gave the line, the decomposition gave R squared, and the F ratio tested the model as a whole.

Today the response is binary. Passed or failed, bought or did not, churned or stayed. Half of what you built last week survives that change and half does not, and the hour is about finding out which half.

Start with the obvious attempt. Fit a straight line to a response that is only ever zero or one, and read what it predicts.

Two things are wrong and the first is visible. On the left the line predicts a negative probability and on the right it predicts one above 1, and neither of those is an unlikely event. They are not events at all.

The second is quieter. For a binary response the conditional variance is p times one minus p, so the spread changes as the fitted probability changes. Last week's standard errors assumed a constant conditional variance.

Divide the first by the second and the denominators cancel. What is left is the odds, and it is simply e to the z.

The model is a straight line in the log odds and not in the probability. An observed zero or one has no finite log odds, so there is nothing for an ordinary least squares regression to regress, and the fitting rule has to change. The coefficients are chosen to make the observed pattern of zeros and ones as likely as the model can make it, which is maximum likelihood.

One question remains from last week. Does the model as a whole earn its input?

📋 Note for Students

Three pieces, and none of them is new machinery.

The null model is the one with no input at all. It predicts the same probability for everybody, which is just the proportion who passed. Its log likelihood is written as l with the subscript null, and it is the worst any honest model should do.

The fitted model uses your input. Its log likelihood is l with the subscript fitted, and it is always at least as large, because the null model is the fitted model with the slope forced to zero.

G squared is twice the gap between them. It is a measure of how much the input bought you, on the scale the fitting rule actually works in. If the input tells you nothing, the gap is near zero. The chi-square curve is what says how large a gap could get by luck alone, and the p-value is the area beyond your G squared.

Section 2 of 5
DEMONSTRATION

No new formulas from here on. One model, fitted to this course's own data, taken apart column by column.

The intercept is minus 25.6019 and the slope is 0.3638. Read across a row and you are doing what the model does.

Look at what each column does as you go down five points at a time. The log odds add the same amount every time, which is five slopes. The odds multiply by the same factor every time. The probability does neither, because it is squeezed into the interval at both ends.

That is the whole model in one sentence. It is a straight line in one column and a curve in another, and the middle column is what connects them.

📋 Note for Students

A binary input works the same way with one difference: there is only one step to take.

When the input can only be 0 or 1, the model has exactly two log odds in it. One for the group coded 0, which is the intercept on its own, and one for the group coded 1, which is the intercept plus the slope. Going from 0 to 1 adds the slope to the log odds, once, and multiplies the odds by e to the slope, once. There is no second step and no table to walk down, because there is nowhere else to go.

So the slope means the same thing in both models. It is what one step of the input does to the log odds. With a midterm score a step is one point, and you can take as many as you like. With a pass or fail flag a step is the whole distance from one group to the other, and you can take it exactly once.
