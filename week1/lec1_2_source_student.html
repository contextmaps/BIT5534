Sampling Distributions: Building the Simulator and Finding the Central Limit Theorem
Duration: 75 minutes · Platform: Hokie AI (build) + local Python (run) · Submission: Thread exported as a .txt and uploaded to Canvas before leaving class · Lecture Mode: exploratory
Learning Objectives

By the end of this lecture, students will be able to:

  1. Define a parameter and a statistic, and explain why a statistic follows a distribution and a parameter does not.
  2. Specify a sampling simulator in plain language, have HokieAI write the Python for it, and run it by double-clicking the file.
  3. Derive the variance of a uniform population and use the derived value to verify the tool before relying on any number it reports.
  4. Check the two relationships that define a sampling distribution, one stating that the mean of the sample means approaches the population mean, the other stating that the variance of the sample means equals the population variance divided by the sample size.
  5. Vary the sample size and describe how the distribution of the sample mean responds.
  6. State what the central limit theorem claims and what condition it requires.
  7. Push an explanation from HokieAI past its first answer across several turns, then describe one specific turn from that exchange.

A bus leaves this stop every ten minutes. Passengers arrive whenever they arrive, without consulting the timetable, so one who turns up just after a departure waits almost ten minutes and one who turns up just before waits almost none. No waiting time is favoured over any other. Record the wait of ten thousand passengers and you have ten thousand values spread uniformly between 0 and 10 minutes.

The full set of 10,000 waits is the population. A number that describes the population, such as its mean or its variance, is a parameter. A parameter does not have a distribution, because a parameter is either a fixed value computed from all observations in the population or a theoretical value obtained from a formula. Parameters are written with Greek letters, μ for the mean and σ² for the variance.

A sample is a set of observations drawn from the population. A number computed from a sample, such as its average, is a statistic, and statistics are written with Roman letters, x̄ for the sample mean. A statistic is a value computed from samples drawn from the population; therefore, it follows a distribution. A statistic that stands in for a parameter whose value is unknown is an estimate.

Today you will draw 1,000 samples of passengers from that population, compute the mean wait in each sample, and examine the distribution formed by those 1,000 averages.

Two claims follow, and you will test both. Read them as claims to be checked.

The first claim states that the mean of the 1,000 sample means approaches the population mean. The second states that the variance of the sample means equals the population variance divided by the sample size.

The first claim you will confirm quickly. The second carries the weight, because it says that averaging reduces variability by a predictable amount, and the square root that follows from it governs how much a given quantity of data is worth.

📋 Note for Students

Two problems can stop a script from running, and each one is fixed once on your machine and then stays fixed. Both are worth knowing now, because they will come back in later lectures.

A missing package. Python arrives with a large standard library, and anything outside it is installed once with pip. If a window tells you a package is missing, open a terminal and install it.

On Windows, press the Windows key, type cmd, and press Enter. Then run:

python -m pip install numpy matplotlib

On macOS, press Command and Space, type terminal, and press Enter. Then run:

python3 -m pip install numpy matplotlib

Use python3 on macOS. Plain python often does not exist there, and typing it returns command not found even when your installation is healthy. Substitute whatever package names the window reported.

Double-clicking opens an editor instead of running the script. Your machine associates .py files with that editor, so Python is never reached. Fix the association once and every later lecture will run on a double-click.

On Windows, right-click the script, choose Open with, then Choose another app, select Python, and tick the box for always using this app.

On macOS, right-click the script, choose Open With, and select Python Launcher.

Telling the two apart is easy. An editor window means the association needs fixing. A blank console window that flashes and closes means a package is missing.

Before you run the tool, determine what the population variance should be. The waits are drawn from a uniform distribution on the interval 0 to 10 minutes. If you do not remember the variance of a uniform distribution, ask HokieAI for the formula and for its derivation, then apply it yourself. Write the number down before you look at any output.

Now run the tool and compare. Your tool computes its variance from the 10,000 values it drew, and the number you derived is the theoretical variance of the distribution those values came from. The two are different quantities and should agree to within about 0.2. A larger gap means the tool does not match the specification, so work with HokieAI to find the cause before going further.

Once the two agree, look at the histograms. The left one shows the population your tool generated. The right one shows 1,000 averages taken from it.

Now examine the shapes.

The population on the left is flat, because passengers arrive without regard to the timetable and no waiting time is favoured over another. The 1,000 sample means on the right are not flat. They concentrate near 5 and thin toward both ends, forming a bell, although no bell-shaped distribution was ever generated.

Click Resample ten times and note what changes. Every bar changes, and the four numbers change by unequal amounts. The population mean and the mean of the sample means stay close without ever matching exactly, and the gap between them moves from click to click. The population variance shifts by under one percent. The variance of the sample means moves by several percent, so your check of the second claim is itself an estimate, limited in precision by the 1,000 samples drawn.

The bell does not change. Ten separate populations have produced it, so the bell is a property of averaging and not a property of any particular set of values.

The result is the central limit theorem. The distribution of the sample mean approaches a normal distribution as the sample size grows, whatever the shape of the population the samples came from. Each of your samples holds ten observations and the bell is already present. A common working rule is that thirty observations suffice for populations that are not badly skewed.

Set the sample size to 30 and click Resample. Then set it to 100. The bell stays centred near 5 and grows narrower each time, and the amount by which it narrows is the subject of the next block.

The theorem requires one condition. The population must have a finite variance. Populations without a finite variance exist, and for those the sample means do not approach a bell at any sample size. Keep the condition in mind, because it is where Tuesday's question about squaring the deviations returns.

Now interrogate the result you produced.

Ask HokieAI why the standard error carries the square root of the sample size and not the sample size itself. The first answer will most likely restate the formula in words, which is not an explanation, so press further.

Ask what would have to be true for the relationship to fail. Ask whether the relationship depends on the shape of the population, and test the answer by changing the population in your script and running it again. Ask what happens as the sample size grows, and what the answer implies about the cost of collecting more data. Ask why the mean of your sample means only approaches the population mean, when it would equal the population mean exactly if the 1,000 samples had partitioned the population instead of being drawn with replacement.
