K-Means Clustering
Duration: 75 minutes · Platform: Hokie AI · Submission: Thread exported as a .zip and uploaded to Canvas before leaving class · Lecture Mode: exploratory
Learning Objectives

By the end of this lecture, students will be able to:

  1. Write the two steps of Lloyd's algorithm and say what each step produces.
  2. Compare the cost of hierarchical clustering against the cost of k means on the same data, and say which method a million rows rules out.
  3. Say why the algorithm always stops, and why where it stops depends on where it started.
  4. Build a k means tool from a specification whose three parts carry forward by different distances.
  5. Read a silhouette curve, name the k it selects, and say how far ahead of the runner up that k actually is.
  6. Compare a cluster profile from k means against the profile hierarchical clustering gave for the same companies, and say what the two methods agree about.
Section 1 of 5
INTRODUCTION
📋 Note for Students

Last Thursday you built a tree out of every pairwise distance in the file. Today you find out what the tree cost, and what you do when the file is too big to pay for one.

For the first fourteen minutes you watch. Four pictures and four formulas, and nothing to paste.

Hierarchical clustering could not start until it knew the distance between every pair of points.

Count them. One point has n minus one distances to the others. Doing that for all n points counts each distance twice.

A million rows makes the left side a trillion distance calculations.

K means, also called Lloyd's algorithm, has two steps and no third step.

Each step consumes what the other one produced. Step one turns centers into sets and step two turns sets into centers.

Both steps lower the same quantity.

One pass measures every point against k centers, so a pass costs n times k. A million rows, ten clusters and ten passes comes to a hundred million.

💬 Discussion

Before the screen:

  • The algorithm has to stop. Does stopping mean it found the best answer?
  • Five centers were picked at random. What would have happened with five different ones?

DEMONSTRATION

Watch the two steps run, one iteration at a time.

The diamonds are the current centers. The five distance columns are step one, measuring every point against every center, and the cluster number beside them picks the smallest. The block of means underneath is step two, computed and waiting for the next iteration to claim it.

Your instructor runs it to convergence, then starts it over from new random centers, more than once.

Section 4 of 5
DISCUSSION

Two questions, and your own tool answers both.

First, run it on distance_test.csv with x and y, k from two to ten, standardize on. Read the silhouette curve and say which k it picks.

Five scores 0.532 and six scores 0.536, so the winner leads by four thousandths and ten random restarts cannot resolve a gap that small.

Look at what is in the sixth cluster. One point, at x of -9.332 and y of 8.863, out on its own in the upper left. An average silhouette rewards separating a point that sits far from everything, so a tie that close breaks toward putting the outlier in a cluster of its own.

Second question. Open public_utilities.csv, take all eight numeric columns, nominate Company as the label, and run it.

Go to the parallel coordinates tab. A cluster is a shape across those eight axes, and the thick line is what the cluster is on average. Read one cluster at a time and say what makes it what it is. High nuclear and nothing else remarkable. High fuel and high peak. A company sitting alone with a profile nothing else matches.

Then open last Thursday's tool on the same file, the same eight columns, the same number of clusters, and put the two pictures side by side.
